# Fairness, privacy and legal notes

**These are engineering notes, not legal advice.** They record how the engine is built to be defensible and what a retailer's HR, legal and data protection teams will want to check. French law, the GDPR and the EU AI Act change, and their application depends on facts this project does not have. Dates, article numbers and interpretations below must be checked with a lawyer or the data protection officer (DPO) before any real deployment.

## 1. What the system keeps and what it never touches

- Pseudonymous worker ids (`W001`...), timings, counts, and the weekly cash discrepancy sheet. No customer data, no item identity, no barcode, no loyalty-card identity: the loyalty card is an event with a duration, not an identifier.
- Absences are not in the data model: no sick leave, maternity, other protected leave. An attendance term, if ever added, may count only unjustified absences (see the [method document](method_scoring.md)), which would be a new input with its own review.
- Tests enforce this: no SQLite table, parquet file or score output may have a column or key named like a customer, item, barcode or absence (`tests/test_fairness.py::test_no_table_or_output_has_absence_or_identity_fields`, `tests/test_storage.py`), the ground-truth table cannot be read through the engine's database connection (`tests/test_pipeline.py`, `tests/test_storage.py`), and the engine source never mentions contract type, tenure in weeks, age, gender or similar fields (`tests/test_fairness.py::test_engine_source_never_references_protected_or_demographic_fields`).
- `contract_type` is never read by the scoring engine: scores are byte-identical when contract type and contract hours are shuffled among workers (`tests/test_pipeline.py::test_contract_type_never_influences_any_score`). Tenure acts only through the `provisional` label (hire date under 4 weeks), which also keeps newcomers out of the reference group the others are compared with; only `pipeline.py` reads `hire_date`, and moving veterans' hire dates earlier changes nothing.

## 2. Safeguards built into the design

- **Decision support, not decisions.** Every record carries `drivers`, `model_version`, an interval and a tier that is withheld when the interval is wide. A manager validates every plan built from the scores; no output changes a contract, pay or a disciplinary file by itself.
- **A worker can see and contest their score.** The review screen shows each person their own score, its main reasons and how it was computed in plain language; `POST /appeals` stores a review request with the model version, and every score response carries an `appeal` field.
- **Review flags are not penalties.** Very high speed together with a high void or manual-entry rate produces a text for a human to look at; it never lowers a score.
- **Newcomers are not buried.** Scores are shrunk toward the average when data is thin, provisional workers are labelled and only compared with other newcomers, and unrated workers are treated as average by the planner interface.
- **No single observation decides.** Rates are decay-weighted over 12 weeks and shrunk, every z-score is capped at plus or minus 3 (`docs/decisions.md`, D-23), tiers are conservative (the whole interval must clear the cutoff).
- **Audit trail.** Every run writes the model version (package, configuration and engine-source hashes), the as-of time and any warnings to an audit log; formulas, weights and thresholds are in `config/default.yaml` and `docs/decisions.md`.
- **Data stays inside the retailer.** The design keeps the event store in the retailer's environment; nothing in this repository sends data anywhere.

## 3. Bias audit (indirect discrimination)

`src/ree/experiments/fairness.py` compares scores between groups at the same hidden skill. The skill is the speed the worker actually showed in the period (for speed and combined scores) and minus the error propensity (for accuracy), so a gap that remains is the engine's doing rather than a real difference in skill, as long as the linear control is adequate. It reports the gap in score points and a relative slope difference. The test suite fails if the default store exceeds the configured limit (`fairness.max_adjusted_gap_points`, 5 points; `fairness.max_slope_gap`, 0.35; at least 5 workers on each side), and a second test plants a 12-point bias to prove the audit would catch it.

Results over 10 independent stores (250 workers, a separate intercept per store, 95 percent intervals; `outputs/reports/results.md`, section "Fairness audit by contract type and tenure"):

- **Contract type (students against full-time): no gap.** Student minus others is +0.22 points for the peak speed score (-0.13 to +0.58), +0.21 off-peak (-0.22 to +0.65), +0.20 on the combined peak score (-0.54 to +0.95) and -0.03 on accuracy (-2.08 to +2.02). All intervals include zero, and the relative slope differences are at most 0.20 in absolute value (the per-store limit is 0.35).
- **Tenure: a small, structured gap for newcomers, inside the limit.** Workers with under 4 weeks of tenure (9 workers) score 4.60 points higher off-peak (3.59 to 5.61) and 2.24 points higher at peak (1.35 to 3.14) than other workers who showed the same speed. Veterans (26 weeks and more, 220 workers) score 1.86 points lower off-peak (-2.49 to -1.23) and 0.99 lower at peak (-1.52 to -0.47) than the rest. Both numbers are within the 5-point limit. The cause is the designed shrinkage: a newcomer with a slow current speed and little data is pulled toward 50, which protects newcomers from being buried but makes their displayed speed score optimistic. The report repeats the audit on the same stores with speed shrinkage switched off (`engine.speed.shrinkage_k` = 0): the newcomers' gaps become -0.67 (-1.40 to +0.05) at peak and -0.44 (-1.16 to +0.28) off-peak, the veterans' +0.05 and -0.15, and the 4 to 12 week band's +0.19 and +0.43, with every interval including zero. Shrinkage is meant to steady scores built on thin data; this audit prices its side effect (a few points of optimism for newcomers) but does not measure the steadiness it buys. The veterans' gap is largely the mirror of the same effect, because the group they are compared with contains the lifted newcomers (the worker-weighted average of the two other audited groups' gaps is about 1.8 points off-peak and 0.9 at peak); provisional workers are outside the reference cohort, so the veterans' own scores do not depend on the newcomers. One worker in the 12 to 26 week band is too few to audit. Combined scores differ by at most 1.7 points between a tenure band and the rest, and every interval includes zero. Newcomers are labelled provisional and are not tiered against veterans.
- **Accuracy by tenure: no level gap, a slope difference worth watching.** The level gaps are not distinguishable from zero (veterans -1.42 points, interval -4.97 to +2.14; 4 to 12 weeks +1.88, interval -1.75 to +5.51). The relative slope gaps are 0.71 for veterans and -0.40 for the 4 to 12 week band, above the per-store limit of 0.35, which means the accuracy score of the thinner-data groups responds less to their true accuracy. That is what shrinkage does, but accuracy is the noisiest score and the groups are small, so the slopes are poorly determined, and the pooled table gives no interval for them. The per-store gate applies the slope limit to the default store only.
- **Limits of the audit.** It needs the hidden skill, which only the simulator has. In a real store the same comparison must use proxies (basket mix, register, hours, period) and a human reading of any gap. Small groups are noisy: a single store gives gaps up to 7.6 points by chance, which is why the pooled estimate is reported.

Other places indirect discrimination can enter, to review with the works council: who gets rush shifts, heavy lanes or the priority lane (the biased scenario in the experiments shows how assignment can leak into naive measures); part-time and student contracts (the audit finds no gap for the engine, but the planner can still create one); the weekly error tolerance (the audit finds no gap by contract type, a different rule might); and any future attendance or manager-rating term.

## 4. France: works council and prior information

- **Works council (CSE).** The Code du travail requires the CSE to be informed and consulted before the introduction of means or techniques to monitor employees' activity (art. L2312-38) and on new technologies and organisation (art. L2312-8). Plan the consultation before go-live, give the council the formulas, weights, thresholds, the bias audit and the list of what is measured, and expect questions about the planner (Part 2).
- **Prior information of employees.** No information about an employee may be collected by a device they were not told about in advance (art. L1222-4), and restrictions on individual rights must be justified by the task and proportionate (art. L1121-1). The [method document](method_scoring.md) already requires telling workers in advance what is measured; document it in writing, and check whether the internal rules (règlement intérieur) or a collective agreement need updating.
- **Contracts and working time.** A score used to allocate shifts or breaks interacts with working-time rules and with equal treatment between part-time and full-time workers; check the planner against them.

## 5. GDPR

- **Controller and basis.** The employer is the controller. The likely lawful basis is legitimate interest (art. 6(1)(f)), which needs a documented balancing test; employee consent is generally not a safe basis. Check national employment-data rules (art. 88).
- **Minimisation and purpose.** Only timings, counts and the weekly cash sheet; no customer data (art. 5(1)(c)). Keep the purpose to rating cashier efficiency for shift planning. Define a retention period for event data and scores.
- **Transparency and rights.** Inform workers (arts. 13 and 14). A worker can see their score, its reasons and the model version (access, art. 15), can contest it (the appeal endpoint), and can ask for correction or object (arts. 16 and 21).
- **Automated decisions (art. 22).** The scores are profiling. Decisions with legal or similarly significant effects must not rest solely on automated processing; the design therefore requires a manager to validate every plan, and Part 2 must keep that human step meaningful (a manager who can see the reasons and the intervals and change the plan).
- **Impact assessment (art. 35).** Systematic evaluation of employees is likely to require a data protection impact assessment before launch; involve the DPO. This repository can supply its inputs (data flows, formulas, the audit, the tests).
- **Security (art. 32).** The demonstration API has no authentication and the review screen writes directly to the database; both must be put behind the retailer's identity and access controls before real use.

## 6. EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689)

- **Likely classification.** Annex III, point 4 (employment and workers management) lists AI systems used to make decisions affecting terms of work-related relationships, to allocate tasks based on individual behaviour, or to monitor and evaluate the performance and behaviour of workers. A system that evaluates cashiers and feeds a shift planner fits that description, and an Annex III system that profiles natural persons is treated as high-risk. Whether a statistical scoring engine meets the Act's definition of an AI system is a question for counsel (art. 3(1) and the Commission's guidance).
- **If high-risk, the usual obligations apply:** a risk management system, data governance, technical documentation, logging (the audit log and model versions are a start), transparency to deployers, human oversight, accuracy and robustness (the calibration results in `outputs/reports/results.md` are an input), and, for the employer deploying it, informing workers' representatives and the affected workers before putting the system into service (art. 26(7)).
- **Dates.** Obligations for Annex III systems were scheduled to apply from 2 August 2026. Secondary sources checked on 4 October 2026 report that the "digital omnibus" amendment, approved by the Council on 29 June 2026, moved the standalone Annex III high-risk deadline to 2 December 2027 ([Cloud Security Alliance research note](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-eu-ai-act-omnibus-vii-deadline-delay-20260/), [Leaseurope summary](https://www.leaseurope.org/new-application-dates-high-risk-ai-system-rules)). These are not primary sources; check the Official Journal and counsel for the dates in force when you launch.

## 7. Known weaknesses to state plainly to a works council

- The 90 percent intervals are too narrow, mostly for the accuracy half of the score: in the simulation, a nominal 90 percent interval contains the replicate mean 78.7 percent of the time (88.5 percent would be perfect), and the accuracy and combined intervals are about 1.4 times too narrow while the speed intervals are close to calibrated. That is why tiers are conservative and why intervals are shown with the scores.
- The accuracy score rests on sparse cash discrepancies and is the noisier half of the combined score; the void rate carries most of the signal.
- Everything measured here is on simulated data with assumed parameters; none of it shows how the engine behaves in a real store.

## 8. Checklist before any real deployment

1. Counsel and DPO review this note and the [method document](method_scoring.md); decide the lawful basis and the AI Act position.
2. Consult the works council with the formulas, the audit and the planner design; inform workers in writing.
3. Run a DPIA; set retention periods; put the API and screen behind authentication.
4. Replace the simulator audit with an audit on real data using proxies; agree the weekly error tolerance with a head cashier or manager.
5. Run the engine in shadow mode (scores visible only to the review group, no planning use) and measure calibration on real data before Part 2 uses it.
